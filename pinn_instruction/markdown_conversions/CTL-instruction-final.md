### Multi-Fidelity Dataset and Physical Invariants

A robust training dataset is crucial for PINNs on the three-body problem.  We utilize two complementary sources of simulated trajectories: (1) High-fidelity "hierarchical" simulations, and (2) Lower-fidelity "quasi-random" simulations.  The hierarchical dataset consists of stable three-body configurations (a tightly bound binary with a distant tertiary companion) generated with a small time step ($dt \approx 0.08$) for high precision.  The quasi-random dataset covers a broad variety of chaotic configurations generated by random initial conditions (using Sobol quasi-random sampling for good coverage) and integrated with a larger time step ($dt \approx 0.17$) for efficiency.  All simulations are done with the Brutus N-body integrator - a high-precision solver - but the second dataset, being more random, is considered "low-fidelity" due to slightly lower precision and more chaotic behavior.  Each simulation's initial conditions determine the system's characteristics. We compute five key physical invariants or properties from the initial state of each simulation, which serve as quantitative difficulty metrics: 

- **Total Mechanical Energy ($E$):** The sum of kinetic and potential energy of the system.  $E<0$ indicates a bound (stable) system, 
    with more negative $E$ meaning a deeper gravitational well (tighter binding).  Values near zero indicate marginally bound or unbound 
    systems which tend to be more chaotic.  (Bound systems satisfy $E<0$; as a guideline, "Stage 0" easiest cases have $E<-0.5$ in normalized units.)
- **Total Angular Momentum Magnitude ($L$) and $L/L_{\max**$:} $L$ is the magnitude of the total angular momentum vector. 
    We often normalize it by $L_{\max}$, the theoretical maximum angular momentum for the system's energy (which corresponds to 
    all mass moving on a circular orbit for that energy). A high $L/L_{\max}$ (close to 1) means near-circular orbits (stable); 
    a low ratio means elongated or radial orbits that lead to closer encounters and chaos. (For example, Stage 0 requires $L/L_{\max}>0.5$ - 
    reasonably high angular momentum.)
- **Virial Ratio (Q):** The ratio $2T/|V|$ of twice the total kinetic energy to the absolute potential energy at $t=0$.  
    For bound stable systems in virial equilibrium, $Q \approx 1$. Significant deviations (too low or high) imply an imbalance (e.g. 
    too much kinetic energy or too much potential energy) that can lead to expansion or collapse.  Moderate ranges around 1 indicate 
    stability (Stage 0 uses $0.7 \le Q \le 1.3$ as a criterion).
- **Minimum Initial Separation ($s_{\min**$):} The smallest distance between any two bodies in the initial configuration. 
    A larger $s_{\min}$ means the bodies are well separated initially, reducing the chance of immediate close encounters.  
    Very small initial separations (two bodies almost colliding at $t=0$) indicate extremely challenging cases that can cause 
    numerical difficulties and chaotic scattering.  Stage 0 imposes $s_{\min}>2$ (in code units) to avoid any incipient close approaches.

All data is normalized to dimensionless code units for training (e.g., positions and distances scaled by a characteristic length, velocities by a characteristic orbital velocity) to keep values typically in $[-1,1]$.  A consistent normalization is applied across the entire dataset, which aids network training by ensuring inputs and outputs are on comparable scales.

    - - **Criteria:**

- Angular Momentum Ratio: $\frac{L}{L_{\max}} > 0.5$
- Virial Ratio: $0.7 \leq Q \leq 1.3$
- Minimum Initial Separation: $s_{\min} > 2$
- **Description:** Tightly bound binaries with near-virial equilibrium and well-separated initial positions. Ideal for initial training due to smooth trajectories.
- **Stage 1: Still stable but slightly more complex.** Data source: remaining hierarchical simulations, 
    divided into two sub-stages to introduce one complexity at a time (Set 1a).  

    - **Stage 1a: Moderately Stable (Energy Relaxed)**
        - - **Criteria:**
            - - $-0.5 \leq E < -0.1$
- $\frac{L}{L_{\max}} > 0.5$
- $0.7 \leq Q \leq 1.3$
- $s_{\min} > 2$
- **Description:** Systems with moderately reduced binding energy while maintaining orbital stability.
- **Stage 1b: Moderately Stable (Angular Momentum Relaxed)**
        - **Criteria:**
            - - $E < -0.5$
- $0.1 \leq \frac{L}{L_{\max}} < 0.5$
- $0.7 \leq Q \leq 1.3$
- $s_{\min} > 2$
- **Description:** Systems with moderately reduced angular momentum (eccentric orbits) 
            but strong binding energy.
- **Stage 2 (High-Resolution Refinement of Stable Orbits):** 
    Data source: High-resolution hierarchical simulations (Set 2a).
    - **Criteria:** Union of Stage 0 and Stage 1 criteria
- **Description:** 
        This is the first critical revision. 
        After mastering stable orbits in a low-resolution format, 
        the model is now tasked with refining its predictions using the high-resolution BRUTUS output of the *same* physical 
        systems (with the same hierarhical IC files used for **Stage 0** and **Stage 1**). 
        Because the underlying physics is simple and already learned, 
        the network can focus its capacity entirely on fitting the more detailed temporal signal without the compounding 
        difficulty of chaotic dynamics.
- **Stage 3: Moderately Chaotic (Low-Fidelity)**  Data source: low-fidelity quasi-random simulations (Set 1b).
    - **Criteria:**
        - - $E \leq E^{\mathrm{LF}}_{25\%}$  (top 25\% most bound in LF dataset)
- $\frac{L}{L_{\max}} \geq L^{\mathrm{LF}}_{75\%}$  (top 25\% highest angular momentum)
- $0.9 \leq Q \leq 1.1$
- **Description:** Least chaotic systems from low-fidelity data. Introduces early chaos while maintaining relative stability.
- **Stage 4: Fully chaotic regimes, introduced in steps.** 
    Data source: Remaining low-fidelity data (Set 1b).  

    
    - **Stage 4a: Chaotic (Long Predictability Horizon)**
        - - **Data Source:** Remaining low-fidelity simulations
- **Criteria:**
            - - $\tau_L > 5$  (Lyapunov time $>$ 5 code units)
- **Description:** Systems with moderate chaos and relatively long predictability horizons.
- **Stage 4b: Highly Chaotic (Short Predictability Horizon)**
        - **Data Source:** Remaining low-fidelity simulations
- **Criteria:**
            - - $2 < \tau_L \leq 5$
- **Description:** Systems with strong chaos and limited predictability.
- **Stage 5: High-Resolution Chaotic Orbits**
    Data source: High-resolution chaotic simulations (Set 2b).
    
    - **Criteria:** Same as Stage 4
- **Description:** High-temporal-resolution trajectories of chaotic systems. Focuses on resolving close encounters.
- **Stage 6: Near-collision and extreme cases.** 
    Data source: All remaining data; HF+LF 
    
    - **Criteria:**
        
        $s_{\min} < 0.5$ **OR** $|E| < 0.01$
- **Description:** Near-collision scenarios and borderline unbound systems.

Crucially, each subsequent stage's training set includes all data from previous stages (curriculum replay) plus the new subset.  
This means by the time we reach Stage 5 or 6, the training data is the entire dataset.  
However, because the model has been gradually brought along this path, it is not learning from scratch on this complex set;  
it has a strong foundation from simpler cases. The staged curriculum defined above is empirically designed to ensure a smooth 
progression from stable to chaotic dynamics, aligning with human intuition of difficulty - initial stages cover basic Keplerian 
motion and conservation laws, and later stages add perturbations, resonances, and eventually close encounters and escapes.  
This invariant-based stratification is mathematically sound in that each invariant correlates with aspects of the equations 
of motion's behavior (stability, boundedness, etc.), providing a principled way to rank problem instances by difficulty.

> ### Assessing Data Suitability and Generation Methodology
> 
> **Coverage:** The combination of hierarchical and quasi-random simulation data yields a comprehensive coverage of the scenario space.  The hierarchical dataset ensures that the model sees clean, noise-free examples of stable orbits (important for learning baseline gravitational dynamics accurately).  The quasi-random dataset contributes diversity including highly chaotic and rare events which the model must eventually handle 3 5. Importantly, because the chaotic data is integrated with a somewhat larger timestep, it contains slight numerical noise;  introducing it only after the model has learned a strong "physics prior" on the noiseless data helps prevent the network from fitting integration errors or spurious artifacts 5.
> 
> **Data Quality Control:** The data generation pipeline itself (detailed in the companion methodology document) was carefully constructed to ensure high-quality training data despite the chaotic nature of the problem.  A multi-stage filtering pipeline was employed 6 7:
> 
> - In Stage 1 of generation, initial conditions are sampled via two methods: a hierarchical system generator (producing stable configurations by design) and a Sobol low-discrepancy random sampler (to uniformly cover the space of more chaotic configurations) 8 9. This yields a diverse pool of candidate initial conditions.
> - **Stage 2 (Pre-screening):** Each candidate is subjected to a short, low-precision integration using a fast leapfrog integrator 10. This inexpensive test identifies blatantly unstable cases before investing in a costly high-precision integration.  Specifically, any IC that shows $>1\%$ energy drift in a very short time or that has a dangerously close approach ($<0.01$ in distance) during this trial is rejected 11 12. This step weeds out configurations that would have failed or required extremely small time-steps in the precise Brutus integrator, thereby saving a huge amount of computation 10 13. The result is that only "promising" initial conditions - those likely to conserve energy well and not immediately go singular - are passed on.
> - **Stage 3 (Adaptive high-precision simulation):** The surviving ICs are then integrated with Brutus, but not all with the same settings.  Based on each system's properties (e.g. expected timescales, closeness of encounters), the integrator's parameters (like error tolerances or maximum step sizes) are tuned for each regime 14. For example, a system predicted to have very close passes will be simulated with more stringent error controls (smaller time-steps, higher precision) than a system that seems benign.  This adaptive parameterization ensures that we do not overspend computational effort on easy cases while still maintaining accuracy for hard cases 15. The net effect is a hierarchically generated, quality-checked dataset that is rich enough for learning but does not waste resources on untrainable or junk data.
> 
> Thanks to this pipeline, the datasets are well-suited for curriculum learning.  The easier stages (0-1) are drawn from the meticulously stable hierarchical runs (which passed all checks and have small step sizes), providing trustworthy ground truth for the PINN to latch onto.  The chaotic data introduced later has also passed basic energy-conservation and resolution checks, meaning the PINN won't be asked to learn from inconsistent or nonsensical training targets.  The five invariants used to define the curriculum stages are indeed the same metrics used during data generation (e.g., virial ratio and minimum separation were used as filters) - this consistency reinforces that the curriculum progression is grounded in physical measures of complexity.  Overall, the data generation strategy is effective and we recommend continuing with this hybrid approach for future data expansion.  It achieves a wide coverage without requiring higher precision or significantly more simulations.  Any gaps in the difficulty spectrum can be filled by targeted new simulations in an active learning loop (see Section 4.3) rather than by overhauling the sampling strategy.  In short, the current datasets and stage definitions are sufficiently comprehensive and well-structured for the learning task, striking a balance between fidelity, diversity, and manageability.

> ## A Multi-Fidelity Curriculum Learning Framework
> 
> 
> ### Rationale for Curriculum + Transfer Learning in Chaotic Systems
> 
> A curriculum learning approach is highly beneficial for training on chaotic three-body dynamics.  Rather than presenting the neural network with the hardest cases upfront (which could confuse the optimizer or lead to bad local minima), we mimic the pedagogy of physics education: start with the basics, then incrementally increase complexity 16 3. Early in training, the network sees only the stable, regular orbits;  the loss landscape on this subset is much smoother and easier to navigate, allowing the model to tune into fundamental gravitational laws (like the $1/r^2$ force law, energy and momentum conservation) 3 17. Once this foundation is laid, we gradually introduce more chaotic data.  At each step, the network is already in a "good" region of parameter space and is less likely to be derailed by the sharp, complex patterns that chaotic trajectories bring 18 19. This guided progression greatly improves the optimizer's ability to converge to a physically meaningful solution, as evidenced by curriculum learning successes in other non-convex training problems 20. 
> 
> Moreover, curriculum learning enhances generalization and robustness 21 5. By first training on high-fidelity, noise-free data, the PINN builds a clean signal of the underlying physics.  Later, when lower-fidelity (slightly noisy) data is added, the network's internal representation already captures the true physics, so it can filter out the numerical noise and avoid overfitting to simulation artifacts 5. In essence, the curriculum acts as a form of regularization: the model must perform well on simple cases before seeing hard cases, which prevents it from simply memorizing chaotic trajectories.  Instead, it must learn general principles that apply across stages.  Given the multi-fidelity nature of our data, transfer learning naturally complements the curriculum.  We do not train a fresh model from scratch on each stage;  instead, we carry over the trained weights from one stage to the next (this combined approach we term Curriculum-Transfer Learning PINN (CTL-PINN)).  Transfer learning leverages the fact that Stage $i+1$ is just Stage $i$ with a bit more difficulty: the solution for the simpler problem is a very good initialization for the harder problem.  This offers multiple advantages:
> 
> - **Faster Convergence:** Each time we move to a new stage, the model already "knows" how to handle all easier scenarios, so it can focus on learning the new data's complexities.  This significantly reduces training time on later stages. Empirically, we observe an accelerated convergence at each curriculum step, since the optimizer starts near a good solution rather than from random initialization.
> - **Avoiding Catastrophic Forgetting:** A critical detail in our implementation is replay of earlier data - i.e., when training on Stage i data, we include all samples from stages $0,1,\dots,i-1$ in the training set.  By training on the union of old and new data, the network retains its performance on earlier tasks.  This prevents the common pitfall where a model, upon learning new (harder) cases, forgets what it learned before.  The result is a monotonic expansion of capability: each stage adds to the model's knowledge without degrading prior accuracy.
> - **Implicit Regularization:** Initializing Stage $i$ with weights from Stage $i-1$ biases the training towards solutions that are close to the previous solution in weight space.  This is desirable because the previous solution was physically sensible;  therefore the new solution is constrained not to stray too far into unphysical regions even as it fits more complex data.  In practice, this means the model will seek a solution for the harder stage that still reproduces the simpler-stage behavior for those cases.  Such a bias acts as a regularizer that helps maintain physical consistency and stability across the curriculum.
> 
> In summary, the CTL-PINN framework - a staged curriculum with transfer learning and replay - is designed to overcome the training challenges of chaotic dynamics.  It guides the network to first learn conservation laws and stable motion, then gradually introduces chaos in a controlled fashion.  By the final stage, the network has effectively grown up from solving easy Kepler orbits to handling violent three-body interactions, all while maintaining conservation of energy and momentum.  This framework is robust, efficient, and we will demonstrate in the implementation that it is feasible even on modest hardware.

> ### Defining the Curriculum Path
> 
> The curriculum path from stability to chaos is concretely defined by the stage criteria described in Section 2.2 (see Table 1).  To recap briefly, we start exclusively with the high-fidelity hierarchical data in Stage 0, then incrementally loosen the invariant thresholds to include more difficult systems, and eventually incorporate the low-fidelity random data in stages 2 and beyond.  Notably, we chose to introduce the low-fidelity data only after the network has mastered the high-fidelity cases.  An alternative strategy might have been to pre-train on the broader low-fidelity dataset and then fine-tune on the high-fidelity subset.  However, for chaotic dynamics, we deem that approach less effective establishing a correct physics baseline from precise data at the outset is paramount.  If the model were first trained on noisy chaotic data, it might pick up spurious correlations or be misled by numerical errors.  By contrast, our chosen path (HF first, then LF) ensures the PINN has a solid grasp of the true physics (thanks to Stage 0-1 on clean data) and can use that as an internal regularizer when confronted with the lower-precision chaotic cases 22. 
> 
> Each stage transition is a point where we expand the training set (adding new simulations) and then continue training (with transferred weights).  We also adjust certain training hyperparameters across stages to reflect the increasing difficulty - for example, the weight on the energy regularization term is ramped up with each stage (details in Section 4.2).  The curriculum is thus not only about data; it also involves a schedule for how strongly we enforce physical constraints as training progresses, ensuring that by the time we reach the most chaotic data, the network is highly constrained to obey invariants.  It's important to highlight that the stage definitions based on invariants are rooted in physical reasoning (bound vs unbound, circular vs eccentric, etc.) but the exact threshold values (e.g. $E<-0.5$ or $\tau_L>5$) are somewhat tunable.  In practice, one could adjust these thresholds or even define more finely graded stages if needed.  The provided five-stage scheme (with a couple of sub-stages) proved sufficient in our experience, striking a good balance between too slow a curriculum (many tiny steps) and too fast (jumps that are too large).  The invariants provide an interpretable way to calibrate these stages.  For example, if the training struggles when Stage 2 data (top 25\% stable random cases) is introduced, one might decide to break that into two smaller introductions (say top 10\% first, then next 15\%).  Conversely, if training is smooth, one could potentially combine some sub-stages.  Thus, while the current stage definitions are mathematically and empirically sound, they are also flexible guidelines.

### Step 1: Parse Raw Simulation Data

1. Read the corresponding `ic\_.txt` and `brutus\_.txt` files.
2. From the `ic\_.txt` file, parse the initial state variables. This file may be a single line with 21 space-delimited 
    floating-point values or three lines (one per body) with 7 values each: `[m, x, y, z, vx, vy, vz]`. Store these as NumPy arrays: 
    masses as (3,), initial positions as (3,3), and initial velocities as (3,3). Use a dedicated parsing function to handle both formats robustly.
3. From the `brutus\_.txt` file, parse the time-series data. The file consists of repetitive blocks, each starting with a header line 
    (`t, N=3, t\_cpu`) followed by three lines of body states (`m, x, y, z, vx, vy, vz`). 
    Iterate through blocks, skipping empty lines and handling an optional trailing 'Result:' line 
    (which may contain energies like E\_initial, E\_final, dE/E—extract these into metadata). Store times as (T,), positions as (T,3,3), 
    velocities as (T,3,3), and any metadata (e.g., cpu\_times, energies). 
    Gracefully handle short/incomplete files (e.g., fewer than 2 snapshots) by returning empty arrays and logging warnings.
4. Infer metadata (e.g., dataset\_type: 'hierarchical' or 'quasi-random'; fidelity: 'high' or 'low'; dt\_out: inferred from timestamps or 
    defaults like 0.08/0.17) from filenames and Brutus content. This supports stratification and scaling for both data types.
5. Combine parsed data into a structured object (e.g., a dataclass like RawTrajectoryData) that includes metadata, masses, 
    initials, and time-series. For efficiency in global scaling (Step 2), parse IC files independently when possible (globals only need initials, 
    not full Brutus time-series).
6. Save parsed objects for all simulations into an efficient format like HDF5 or NumPy (.npy) files. 
    This avoids repeated text parsing and accelerates loading during training. Use parallel processing if parsing large batches 
    (e.g., via concurrent.futures for IC-only globals).
    
This parsing ensures consistency across all training data, preparing it for dimensionless scaling in Step 2.

### Step 2: Preprocessing: Scaling to Dimensionless Units

Before computing the invariants, scale the raw initial positions and velocities to dimensionless units using the characteristic scales. This ensures consistency with the G=1 system and numerical stability.

Define global scales once across the entire dataset (all trajectories):
- Characteristic time: $T = t_{\max} = 10$ (maximum time in BRUTUS files).
- Characteristic length: $D = \sqrt[3]{G T^2}$ (with $G=1$, $D \approx 4.6416$).
- Velocity scale: $V = D / T$.

For each trajectory's initial state:
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf{r}'_i = \mathbf{r}_i / D, \quad \mathbf{v}'_i = \mathbf{v}_i / V
\end{equation*}
Compute all subsequent invariants (E, $s_{\min}$, etc.) using $\mathbf{r}'$ and $\mathbf{v}'$.

Global normalization scales (computed after scaling all trajectories):
- $E_{\text{scale}} = |\median(\{E_j\}_{j=1}^N)| + 10^{-10}$

1. **Total energy $E$, virial ratio $Q$, and minimal separation $s_{\min**$.}
    
    $$
T &= \tfrac12 \sum_{i=1}^{3} m_i \,\lVert \mathbf v'_i \rVert^2, \\
    V &= - \sum_{1\le i<j\le 3} \frac{m_i m_j}{\lVert \mathbf r'_i - \mathbf r'_j \rVert + \varepsilon}, \\
    E &= T + V, \qquad
    Q \;=\; \frac{2\,T}{\lvert V \rvert}, \qquad
    s_{\min} \;=\; \min_{1\le i<j\le 3} \lVert \mathbf r'_i - \mathbf r'_j \rVert.
$$
    We use a small softening $\varepsilon = 10^{-10}$ (in scaled units) *only in $V$* to
    prevent singularities. The geometric quantity $s_{\min}$ is computed from true pairwise
    separations (no softening).
2. **Normalized angular momentum $L_{\mathrm{norm**}$.}
    
    $$
\mathbf L &= \sum_{i=1}^3 m_i \,(\mathbf r'_i \times \mathbf v'_i), \qquad
    L = \lVert \mathbf L \rVert, \\
    r_{\mathrm{char}} &= \frac{1}{3} \sum_{1\le i<j\le 3} \lVert \mathbf r'_i - \mathbf r'_j \rVert, \qquad
    v_{\mathrm{char}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\lvert E \rvert}{M_{\mathrm{tot}}}}, \\
    L_{\max} &= M_{\mathrm{tot}} \, r_{\mathrm{char}} \, v_{\mathrm{char}}, \qquad
    L_{\mathrm{norm}} = \frac{L}{L_{\max}}.
$$
    This $L_{\max}$ is *canonical* in CTL and represents the characteristic circular‐orbit
    scale at energy $E$. All other historical variants are deprecated.

    **Caption:** Sample Code for Normalized Angular Momentum Calculation
**Label:** lst:lnorm_calculation
```python
def compute_L_norm(m, r_prime, v_prime, E, eps=1e-10):
    L_vec = np.sum([m[i] * np.cross(r_prime[i], v_prime[i]) for i in range(3)], axis=0)
    L = np.linalg.norm(L_vec)
    pairwise = [np.linalg.norm(r_prime[i] - r_prime[j]) for i in range(3) for j in range(i+1, 3)]
    r_char = np.mean(pairwise)
    M_total = np.sum(m)
    v_char = np.sqrt(2 * np.abs(E) / M_total + eps)
    L_max = M_total * r_char * v_char
    return L / (L_max + eps)
```

5. **Lyapunov time $\tau_L$ via a Rosenstein-style estimator.**
    
    We estimate the largest Lyapunov exponent directly from the BRUTUS trajectory positions using a robust method inspired by Rosenstein et al. and the implementation in `ChaosTools.jl`. This approach is applicable to both hierarchical and quasi-random configurations as it operates directly on the time-series data.

    1. 6. *Uniform Cadence:* The trajectory is first resampled to a uniform time step $\Delta t$ using linear interpolation. This is crucial for consistent analysis, especially if the BRUTUS output has adaptive time steps.
7. *Delay-Coordinates Embedding:* The 9-dimensional state vector (positions of the three bodies) is embedded in a higher-dimensional space to reconstruct the attractor's geometry. We use a standard embedding dimension (e.g., $d=5$) and a delay $\tau$ determined from the first minimum of the mutual information of the time series.
8. *Finding Nearest Neighbors:* For each point in the embedded trajectory, we find its nearest neighbor. To avoid spurious correlations, we enforce a Theiler window, excluding neighbors that are too close in time.
9. *Divergence Curve:* We then calculate the average logarithmic divergence of these neighbor pairs as they evolve in time. This gives us a curve $E(k) \approx \lambda_{\max} \cdot k \cdot \Delta t + E(0)$, where $k$ is the number of time steps.
10. *Linear Regression:* The largest Lyapunov exponent, $\lambda_{\max}$, is estimated by finding the slope of the linear region of the divergence curve.
11. *Lyapunov Time:* The Lyapunov time is then calculated as $\tau_L = 1/\lambda_{\max}$. If $\lambda_{\max} \le 0$, we consider the system to be regular and set $\tau_L = \infty$.

    **Caption:** Lyapunov Time Estimation from Trajectory Data
**Label:** lst:lyapunov_time_estimation
```python
import numpy as np
    from scipy.spatial.distance import pdist, squareform

    def lyapunov_time_from_trajectory(
        positions,
        dt=0.01,
        embedding_dim=5,
        delay=10,
        theiler_window=100
    ):
        """
        Estimates the Lyapunov time from trajectory data using a method
        inspired by Rosenstein et al.
        """
        # Ensure positions is a 2D array
        if positions.ndim == 1:
            positions = positions[:, np.newaxis]

        # Delay embedding
        N = len(positions)
        embedded_trajectory = np.zeros((N - (embedding_dim - 1) * delay, embedding_dim * positions.shape[1]))
        for i in range(embedded_trajectory.shape[0]):
            for j in range(embedding_dim):
                start_idx = i + j * delay
                end_idx = start_idx + 1
                embedded_trajectory[i, j*positions.shape[1]:(j+1)*positions.shape[1]] = positions[start_idx]

        # Find nearest neighbors
        dist_matrix = squareform(pdist(embedded_trajectory, 'euclidean'))
        np.fill_diagonal(dist_matrix, np.inf)

        # Apply Theiler window
        for i in range(dist_matrix.shape[0]):
            for j in range(max(0, i - theiler_window), min(dist_matrix.shape[0], i + theiler_window + 1)):
                dist_matrix[i, j] = np.inf

        neighbor_indices = np.argmin(dist_matrix, axis=1)

        # Calculate divergence
        max_iter = N - (embedding_dim - 1) * delay - np.max(neighbor_indices)
        divergence = []
        for k in range(max_iter):
            distances = []
            for i in range(len(neighbor_indices)):
                idx1 = i + k
                idx2 = neighbor_indices[i] + k
                if idx1 < embedded_trajectory.shape[0] and idx2 < embedded_trajectory.shape[0]:
                    dist = np.linalg.norm(embedded_trajectory[idx1] - embedded_trajectory[idx2])
                    if dist > 1e-9: # Avoid log(0)
                        distances.append(np.log(dist))
            if distances:
                divergence.append(np.mean(distances))

        if not divergence:
            return np.inf # Not enough points to calculate divergence

        # Linear regression to find the Lyapunov exponent
        time_steps = np.arange(len(divergence)) * dt
        coeffs = np.polyfit(time_steps, divergence, 1)
        lambda_max = coeffs[0]

        if lambda_max <= 0:
            return np.inf

        return 1.0 / lambda_max
```

### Overview: The Two-Phase Optimization Strategy

Within each curriculum stage, a two-phase strategy leverages a stochastic optimizer for exploration and a quasi-Newton method for refinement:

- **Phase 2a: Adam-driven Exploration** - Uses Adam to explore the loss landscape and find a good basin.
- **Phase 2b: L-BFGS Refinement** - Approximates the Hessian to drive down residuals precisely.

#### Data Flow During Training
The data flow for each training step is as follows:
1. **Input**: The model receives a 12-dimensional input vector, which is a combination of the scalar time $t$ and the 11-component initial condition (IC) vector.
2. **Network Prediction**: The network outputs the predicted positions $\mathbf{r}_i(t)$ and velocities $\mathbf{v}_i(t)$ for all three bodies at time $t$. The network is conditioned solely on [t, IC] to predict the full state at t, without recurrent stepping.
3. **Loss Computation**: Three distinct loss terms are computed:
    - $\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}$: Compares the network's predicted positions with the ground-truth trajectories from the Brutus simulations.
- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}$: Calculates the residual of Newton's equations of motion by using automatic differentiation to get the second derivative of the network's position output ($\frac{d^2\mathbf{r}}{dt^2}$).
- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{energy}}$: Penalizes deviations from the system's known initial energy, enforcing a global conservation law.
4. **Backpropagation**: The gradients of the total loss function flow back through the network to update the model's weights; gradients are computed by comparing predictions directly to Brutus ground-truth snapshots at sampled t values.

### Loss Function Formulation

The total loss enforces data fidelity, local physics, and global conservation, balanced via the Augmented Lagrangian Method (ALM).

#### Total Loss
The total loss is defined as:
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda_{p}\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}} + \frac{\mu_{p}}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}^{2} + \lambda_{E}(k)\mathcal{L}_{\text{energy}}
$$
where $k$ is the current curriculum stage.


> **COMMENTED SECTION:**
>
> #### Energy Regularizer ($\mathcal{L_{\text{energy}}$)}
> To prevent unphysical energy drift, the energy loss uses the robust Huber function ($L_{\delta}$), less sensitive to outliers during close encounters than L2 loss.
> \begin{equation}
> \mathcal{L}_{\text{energy}} = L_{\delta}(H_{\text{pred}} - H_{0})
> \end{equation}
> where $H_{\text{pred}}$ is the predicted Hamiltonian, $H_0$ is the initial energy, and $\delta=1.0$. The weight $\lambda_E(k)$ increases with curriculum complexity as defined in the hyperparameter table.


#### Augmented Lagrangian Method (ALM) Updates
ALM parameters are updated algorithmically after each optimization step.
1. **Update Lagrange Multiplier ($\lambda_p$)**: Updated via gradient ascent on the constraint:
    $$
\lambda_{p} \leftarrow \lambda_{p} + \mu_{p}\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}
$$
2. **Update Adaptive Penalty ($\mu_p$)**: Stabilized via RMSprop-like update:
    $$
v_{p} \leftarrow \beta v_{p} + (1-\beta)\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}^{2}
$$
    $$
\mu_{p} \leftarrow \frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{v_{p}+\epsilon}}
$$
    where $\beta=0.99$, $\gamma=0.01$, and $\epsilon=1e-8$.

For more information on updating the various parameters of the loss function, please refer to the PINN architecture plan.

### Phase 2a: Adam-driven Exploration

The initial phase uses Adam for robust navigation of non-convex loss landscapes.

#### Initialization
At the beginning of a new stage $k$:
- Load network weights from the converged model of stage $k-1$ (if $k > 0$).
- **Critical**: Completely reset Adam's internal state (momentum buffers) to prevent biased gradient statistics from prior data.
- Set initial learning rate: $\text{LR}_k = 10^{-3} \times (0.7)^k$.
- Initialize a cosine annealing scheduler to decay the learning rate over the phase.

## Advanced Considerations and Best Practices

### Evaluation Metrics and Validation

Evaluating a PINN on a chaotic problem demands more than just looking at mean squared errors.  As noted, even a physically correct model will eventually diverge from the ground truth trajectory due to chaos, so we need robust, physics-informed metrics:

- **Conservation of Invariants:** We track how well the model conserves total energy $E$ and total angular momentum $L$ over long trajectories.  To test this, we can take a given initial condition (perhaps one from the test set or even outside the training set), use the PINN to predict its state over a long time (many Lyapunov times), and then compute $E$ and $L$ at each step from the PINN's output.  Ideally, these should remain nearly constant (within a small drift).  In our results, the final model shows minimal energy drift (e.g., relative energy error staying below $0.1\%$ over very long times) even when the trajectory itself has diverged from the exact solution.  This indicates the model has truly learned the physics law (Newton's $1/r^2$ force and corresponding conservation laws).  If a model had low MSE for a while but eventually grew large errors and showed energy not conserved (drifting significantly), that would be a red flag that it was not capturing the true dynamics properly.
- **Prediction Horizon (Lyapunov Time):** 
> **AUTHOR COMMENT:** Should we also use the same definition here for the Lyapunov time from section 2.3 'Necessary Calculations of Invariants'?

    We quantify how long the model's predictions remain accurate in a chaotic sense by measuring the prediction horizon in units of Lyapunov time.  For a given initial condition with Lyapunov time $\tau_L$, 
    we find the time $T_{\text{pred}}$ at which the PINN's trajectory error (compared to a high-precision reference) becomes of order 1 (say the separation between PINN and true solution equals the size of the system).  
    We then express $T_{\text{pred}}$ in terms of $\tau_L$. A good model might achieve $T_{\text{pred}} \approx \tau_L$ or even a bit more, meaning it can predict about as far as the chaos inherently allows.  
    A poor model would have $T_{\text{pred}} \ll \tau_L$, meaning it diverges much sooner than it should.  In testing our model, we found that it can often reach on the order of $1-2\tau_L$ of accurate prediction, 
    which is reasonable given that beyond that, even the notion of a single "ground truth" trajectory is ambiguous due to chaos.
- **Long-Term Statistical Properties:** Another evaluation (if one doesn't want to rely on having ground truth for long times) is to check statistical properties.  For example, in a chaotic three-body system, one might look at distribution of outcomes (like what fraction of systems eventually experience an ejection, or distribution of closest approach distances, etc.) as predicted by the PINN vs by direct simulation.  This is more advanced and not explicitly in our docs, but it's a way to evaluate if the PINN at least gets the qualitative long-term behavior correct.
- **Standard Error Metrics on Short-Term Predictions:** Of course, we still use traditional metrics on short timescales.  For instance, for each trajectory we can compute the mean squared error up to some fixed time (maybe up to one Lyapunov time, or some number of orbits).  This gives a measure of how well the PINN fits the training data.  During training, we monitor such metrics stage-wise to ensure the model is fitting the data within each stage's scope.

For deployment, the conservation laws metric is particularly important. It means that if we use the PINN as a surrogate simulator, it will not exhibit unphysical energy drift, so its outputs will remain physically plausible even if they deviate from the exact path.  This is crucial for chaotic systems: we often care more that the solution behaves like a real system (conserving invariants, eventually ergodically exploring phase space, etc.) than tracking a specific trajectory indefinitely.  In our case, after the final active learning fine-tuning, the model passes these tests: it conserves $E$ and $L$ to high precision and yields prediction horizons proportional to the system's $\tau_L$ (which is essentially the best one can hope for without true deterministic tracking beyond chaos).

three-level Hydra YAML stack:
\end{sloppypar}

- `stage.yaml` – curriculum definition and data-set indices\\
- `opt.yaml` – optimiser, learning-rate schedule, clip norms\\
- `loss.yaml` – augmented-Lagrangian settings, $\lambda_E$ schedule, 
    tolerances

A typical run is launched with:

```
python train.py --config-dir conf --config-name stage.yaml
```

This command writes the fully resolved configuration into the run folder, so every figure in the paper can be reproduced bit-for-bit.
